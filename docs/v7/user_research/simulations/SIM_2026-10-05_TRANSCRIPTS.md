## S1 — ML engineer, B2B SaaS support RAG bot

1. I own the customer-support chatbot for our B2B SaaS product. It's a RAG setup: a vector DB over our help center and billing docs, plus a hosted LLM API that writes the answers.
2. About seven weeks ago we moved to the newer model version from the same provider, mostly because the old one was being deprecated. A couple of weeks later, billing answers started going wrong. The bot would say things like "you'll be prorated automatically" for annual plans, which isn't true for us, or it would mix up seat-based and usage-based pricing. Other topics looked fine as far as we could tell. It was really just billing.
3. Honestly, we didn't notice it ourselves. Support escalated a handful of tickets where customers quoted the bot back at them, and I think it was roughly 10 to 12 days after the switch before someone connected them. Our CI evals passed on the switch. In hindsight that's because only about five of our ~80 eval cases were billing questions, and they were the easy ones.
4. First I opened LangSmith and pulled the traces for the conversations from the escalated tickets. I checked what got retrieved, and the right billing chunks were mostly there, which surprised me. Next I took those same questions and replayed them in a notebook against the old model version, which we could still call at that point. The old model answered them correctly with the same retrieved context. After that I spent quite a while diffing prompts and outputs by hand, trying to see what the new model was doing with the context.
5. The side-by-side comparison took the longest, because LangSmith shows me one trace at a time and I was basically eyeballing two browser tabs. The guesswork was working out why the new model got it wrong. My theory was that it leaned more on its own prior knowledge of "typical SaaS billing" and less on our retrieved chunks, especially when two chunks conflicted a bit. That was a theory, though. I never really proved it.
6. I decided it was the model and not retrieval, because retrieval was identical across both versions in the replays and only the model changed. So I was fairly sure it was "model version plus our prompt", maybe 80%. I was much less sure about the mechanism. One of our billing docs also had an outdated section about proration that was still in the index, and I still can't fully say whether the new model was just more willing to pick up that stale chunk.
7. Sort of. I tightened the system prompt to say "only answer billing questions from the provided context, otherwise hand off to a human" and deleted the stale proration section. Then I wrote about 25 new billing eval cases, mostly from the real complaints, and checked that they passed. Support also told us the escalations dropped off over the following two weeks. That's reassuring, but it isn't really proof.
8. My team lead, and the head of support, who was pretty annoyed. I showed them a spreadsheet: question, old-model answer, new-model answer, fixed answer, for maybe 15 examples. That convinced them enough. Nobody asked for anything more formal. No auditor was involved, and I don't think a customer ever saw an explanation beyond "we've fixed it".
9. The main thing was customers getting wrong billing information. At least a couple disputed invoices based on what the bot told them, and finance had to deal with that. Support also spent extra time cleaning up, and for about a week they basically told people not to trust the bot on billing. I don't think it affected any bigger business decisions, but it hurt trust in the bot internally.
10. Telling a customer something wrong about money, in writing, that they can screenshot. If the bot had promised a refund or a discount we don't offer, that could have turned into a real commercial argument. For me personally, the risky part was that our evals said everything was fine, so for a while it looked like we just hadn't been paying attention.
11. LangSmith for traces, our CI eval suite (pytest plus an LLM-as-judge scorer), a Jupyter notebook for the replays, and the support ticket system to find the bad conversations. Plus a lot of Slack.
12. Mostly, why the new model behaved differently given the same inputs. The tools showed me what happened, not why. I also had no easy way to say "here are 50 runs on the old version and 50 on the new one, show me systematically where they differ". I had to build that comparison myself in the notebook. And nothing would have warned us that our eval set barely covered billing.
13. We added the billing eval cases. I also wrote a small script that replays a fixed set of production questions against any candidate model and dumps the outputs side by side, which took me maybe a day. We looked at a couple of commercial eval/monitoring products but didn't buy one. Partly budget, and partly because I wasn't convinced they'd have caught this either. Even with the replay script, I still have to read the diffs myself and decide what matters.
14. A real noticeable regression like this, maybe two or three times a year. Smaller weird stuff comes up more often but usually isn't worth chasing. This one was roughly 25 to 30 hours of my time spread over about a week and a half, plus support and finance time I can't really estimate.

Teams in regulated or money-adjacent areas like fintech or insurance would probably care more than we do. Also, maybe talk to support leads and not only engineers. They're the ones who actually notice first.

## S2 — Solo founder-engineer, tool-using booking agent

1. I'm building an agent that books and reschedules appointments for small service businesses. It reads the customer's message, checks the calendar API, makes or moves the booking, and sends a confirmation through our email API.
2. About three weeks ago, some customers started getting two confirmation emails for one booking. In a few cases the agent sent a "your appointment is confirmed" email before it had actually moved the calendar slot. That same week I'd changed the system prompt so it sounded friendlier and handled "can we push it to Thursday" better. I'd also bumped our agent framework by a minor version because of an unrelated bug fix. So two things changed at once, and that turned out to be most of the problem.
3. A customer told one of our pilot businesses, and they emailed us something like "your bot is spamming my clients." Before that, nothing told me. I don't have alerting on tool-call counts, only logs nobody reads unless something breaks. My best guess is it had been happening for about four or five days before we heard, but I can't say exactly, because I only kept about a week of logs.
4. First I grepped the logs for that customer's email address to find the conversation. My print statements dump each tool call name and arguments, so I could see `send_email` twice in a row with almost the same body. Then I copied the conversation into a notebook and replayed it against the model with the new prompt, and it doubled maybe one run in four. After that I pinned the framework back to the old version and re-ran, then swapped the old prompt back in and re-ran, so basically a dirty little 2×2 by hand. I also read the framework changelog, which said nothing useful about tool calling.
5. The 2×2 re-runs took the longest. The behaviour was flaky, so I had to run each combination something like 15–20 times to believe anything, and with a bit of setup in between that ate most of an afternoon. The guesswork was whether the framework had changed how it serialises tool results back into the context. The changelog didn't say, so I ended up diffing the raw request payloads by eye in the notebook.
6. With the old framework and the new prompt, doubles were rare. With the new framework and the new prompt, they were common. With the new framework and the old prompt, they were somewhere in between. Diffing the payloads, it looked like the new version now added an empty assistant message after a tool result. My new prompt also had a line like "always confirm with the customer by email," and together those seemed to make the model think it hadn't confirmed yet. I'd put it at about 70% sure. The sample sizes were tiny and I never found the actual line in the framework source that changed.
7. Sort of. I reworded the prompt to "send exactly one confirmation email after the calendar update succeeds" and added a dumb guard in code that blocks a second `send_email` to the same address within 60 seconds. Then I re-ran the bad conversation and maybe a dozen others about 20 times each, and saw zero doubles. Honestly, the code guard is what I actually trust. The prompt fix I just hope works.
8. My co-founder, who handles the customers, wanted to know whether it was fixed and whether it would happen again. I showed her the before and after counts from the notebook runs in a Slack message, plus the guard code. The pilot business only got a short "we found it, here's what we changed" email, and that was enough for them. Nobody asked for anything rigorous. Nobody was auditing us.
9. Mostly the end customers getting duplicate or premature emails, which is embarrassing and confusing for them. The premature confirmation is the bad one, because one person turned up for a slot that hadn't actually been moved. We also had one pilot business say they'd pause the agent for a few days, and that hurt because we're trying to turn them into paying customers. Internally it was really just me losing about two days of feature work.
10. The premature "confirmed" email before the calendar write, without question. A duplicate email is annoying, but telling someone they're booked when they aren't erodes trust in the whole product. If that had hit a business that books, say, medical or legal appointments, it would've been a lot worse than annoying. The other risk is softer: I wasn't sure I'd found the real cause, so I couldn't promise it wouldn't come back.
11. Plain Python logging to stdout, which goes into our cloud provider's log viewer with short retention. Then a Jupyter notebook where I paste conversations and re-run them, git to see what changed in the prompt, and the framework's changelog on GitHub. No tracing platform and no eval suite, apart from about ten hand-written test conversations I run before deploys, and those didn't catch it.
12. The logs told me what the agent did, but not the exact context the model saw at each step. Since the framework builds that payload, that was the thing that had changed. Nothing told me whether a bad run and a good run differed because of my prompt, the framework, or just sampling noise. I also didn't have a clean "good" run from before the change saved anywhere to compare against. I was reconstructing it from memory and old logs.
13. I tried the free tier of one of the hosted LLM observability tools a few months ago. It was fine for looking at traces, but the pricing jumped quickly once you went past the free event limit. I also didn't love sending customer emails and calendar details to a third party without a proper review, so I turned it off. Since this incident I've started logging the full request payload to a file per conversation, which is crude but helps. I also froze dependency versions so upgrades become a deliberate step. Neither of those tells me *why* something changed, just *that* it did.
14. Something noticeably weird happens maybe once a month, but most of those are small, like a tone change or a slightly wrong time zone phrasing. Something this bad, where customers are affected, has happened maybe two or three times in about a year. This one took roughly 10–12 hours of my time across two days, counting the replays, the fix and the follow-up messages. That's a rough estimate, I didn't track it.
15. Probably other solo or two-person engineering teams at small startups shipping tool-using agents, especially ones whose agent sends things to the outside world, like emails, messages or payments, where a wrong action costs more than a wrong answer. People who upgrade their agent framework often would also be good, since minor versions in that space seem to change behaviour without much warning. And maybe agencies building agents for clients, because they have to explain incidents to someone else.

My doubts: I'd need it to be very cheap and easy to drop in, ideally self-hosted or local, because I'm not paying per-event pricing. ## S3 — MLOps engineer at a bank (regulated)

1. I run the platform side of two internal LLM systems at the bank. One is a document classifier that routes incoming internal documents, and the other is a retrieval assistant that analysts use to look up internal policy and procedure.
2. A few months ago we re-indexed the internal document store with a new chunking setup. We went from section-aware splitting to fixed-size chunks with overlap, mainly to improve recall on long procedure documents. After that, the assistant started citing superseded policy documents. They looked plausible and were well written, but they were the old versions. Nothing crashed and latency was fine. The answers were simply wrong in a way that looked right.
3. An analyst noticed it, not us. They saw an answer quoting a threshold they knew had changed, and they raised a ticket. By my estimate that was around eight or nine days after the re-index went live. Our quality dashboards didn't move, because our eval set mostly asks about things that only have one version.
4. First I opened the trace for the flagged question in our self-hosted Langfuse and looked at which chunks were retrieved. I could see the old document IDs right there, but not why they had been retrieved. Then I went through our deployment log to list everything that had changed in that window. Besides the re-index there was a prompt template tweak and a minor library upgrade, so I had three suspects. After that I spent a lot of time in a notebook querying the vector store directly with the same question, comparing the scores and metadata of what came back.
5. Ruling out the prompt change and the library upgrade took longest, because I didn't have a clean way to re-run the old configuration against the old index. We hadn't kept a snapshot of the previous index, which in hindsight was our own mistake. The guesswork was mostly about whether the cause was the chunking itself or something else that changed during the re-index.
6. In the end I found that the new chunking job didn't carry over a document-level metadata field we use to mark policies as current or superseded. Our retrieval filter treats a missing field as "pass," so the filter had silently stopped doing anything. I was fairly sure once I counted it: every superseded document that showed up in answers had chunks with that field missing. Even so, I couldn't completely rule out that the larger chunks were also shifting rankings, so I'd describe the confidence as high on the main cause and moderate on whether it was the only one.
7. Yes, but it was clunkier than I'd like. We patched the chunker to propagate the field and changed the filter to fail closed when the field is missing. Then we re-indexed into a staging environment and ran the analyst's original question plus about forty hand-written questions about policies we knew had multiple versions. We also kept a closer eye on the analyst tickets for a couple of weeks after redeploying to production.
8. Yes, model-risk. They needed a written root-cause analysis before we could close the incident and before the fixed index could be promoted. I showed them the before-and-after retrieval results for the test questions, the metadata counts, the code diff of the chunker, and a timeline. Their main pushback was that we had no reproduction of the original faulty state, only my reconstruction of it. That cost us another round of review, and the whole sign-off took roughly two to three weeks after we'd found the cause.
9. Analysts kept using the assistant during that period. Some of them may have relied on outdated thresholds in internal memos or reviews. We had to send a notice asking people to re-check anything they had drafted with the assistant's help since the re-index date. As far as I know nothing customer-facing was affected, but I can't say that with complete certainty, which is exactly the uncomfortable part. The compliance team also had to log it as an internal incident.
10. The riskiest part was not being able to say exactly which answers had been affected during those days. We could list which queries retrieved superseded chunks, but not which of those answers someone actually acted on. For a bank, saying "we think the impact was limited" is not a comfortable position to be in with model-risk or audit.
11. Self-hosted Langfuse for traces, our internal application logging, the deployment and change log, a Jupyter notebook against the vector store, and a small eval suite that runs in CI. The RCA itself was a document written by hand, with screenshots pasted in.
12. The traces showed me what was retrieved but not the state of the index at that moment, meaning which index version, which chunker config, or which metadata the chunks had. Nothing told me directly that a filter had effectively become a no-op. I also had no way to replay the same request against the old configuration and get a side-by-side comparison I could hand to someone else.
13. After the incident we built a few things ourselves. We now snapshot index builds and tag each trace with an index version, and we added metadata-completeness checks to the re-indexing pipeline. We also wrote a regression set specifically for multi-version documents. That helps with this exact failure mode, but it's all reactive. It catches the bug we've already had, and the comparison and evidence-packaging part is still manual. We haven't paid for an external tool, partly because anything that sees prompts or documents means a long procurement and data-protection review.
14. Something big enough to need a formal RCA happens maybe two or three times a year, as a rough estimate. Smaller "why did it answer differently" questions come up more like monthly. The last incident took me, very roughly, 30 to 40 hours of my own time across the investigation and the RCA drafts, plus a few hours each from model-risk, a data engineer and our team lead.
15. Other platform or MLOps people at regulated firms like banks and insurers would be worth talking to, as would people who run RAG over document stores that change often. I'd also suggest talking to someone on the model-risk or validation side, because they're the ones consuming the evidence and they see it from the opposite direction.
16. Partly, yes. The diff and controlled re-runs are the parts that would have helped most. Showing model-risk the first step where a good and a bad run diverge, and then a reproducible re-run, is exactly what they asked for and we couldn't provide. But I have doubts. In our case the run steps were arguably identical, and what differed was the index state underneath. So it only helps if the trace captures index version and chunk metadata, not just inputs and outputs. "Privacy-preserving" also needs to mean concrete things for us: fully on-prem, nothing leaves our network, and we control what's hashed versus stored. Model-risk would also have to accept the evidence bundle's format as valid, and I can't promise they would.
17. In principle, yes, but not quickly and not informally. I'd build it from scratch on public documents, for example published versions of public regulations where older and newer editions both exist. I'd use a toy pipeline that reproduces the chunker problem, with nothing derived from our internal data or code. Even so, I'd need my line manager's approval, and probably a quick check with compliance that sharing it doesn't reveal anything about our architecture. I'd also want a short written agreement on how it would be used. Realistically that's a few weeks of elapsed time, and maybe a day or two of my actual effort.

## S4 — Staff engineer, large consumer app (skeptic)

1. I lead the team that builds LLM features in our consumer app: a summarizer for message threads and smart replies, used by a few million people a day across a few dozen locales.
2. The last real one was a few months ago. Someone changed a shared prompt template so the system instructions could take a new formatting block. For one locale, I think it was Brazilian Portuguese, the localized instruction string got concatenated without a newline, and the model began answering smart replies in English about a third of the time. Every other locale was fine. The evals passed because our pt-BR golden set was small and mostly tested summarization, not replies.
3. We didn't notice it, the language-mismatch metric did. We have a cheap language-ID check on outputs. It's a dashboard metric, not a deploy gate, and it crept up over about a day and a half as the canary widened to full rollout. A regional PM flagged a few user reports at about the same time. So it took roughly 36 hours, and in hindsight that was too slow.
4. I opened the APM vendor's LLM view first, filtered to the reply feature and locale, and confirmed the spike lined up with a release window. Next I went to the prompt registry and diffed the template versions shipped in that window. There were two candidate changes, and one of them touched the shared template. I pulled about twenty bad outputs, looked at the rendered prompts next to them, and saw the mangled instruction line almost right away. Then I reverted in the registry, which takes a few minutes for us.
5. The longest part was before any of that: the 36 hours before anyone was looking. Once we were looking, it took maybe two hours to understand. The guesswork was deciding whether it was our change or the model provider shifting something under us, because there was a minor provider-side model update that same week. For about 30 minutes we argued about which one it was.
6. Revert-and-observe settled it. We rolled the template back for pt-BR only, held the model version fixed, and the mismatch rate fell to baseline within a couple of hours. Then I replayed about 200 logged inputs offline against both template versions and got the same split. That gave me high confidence, because a clean A/B on one variable is about as good as it gets.
7. Yes. Production metrics returned to baseline, and the offline replay showed the fixed template matching the old behaviour. Then we did the thing that actually mattered: we added a reply-specific pt-BR eval slice and promoted the language-ID check to a blocking gate in canary. Honestly, that was the real fix. The template bug itself was trivial.
8. My director and the localization lead, but it wasn't an adversarial conversation. I showed them the metric graph, the registry diff with the bad line highlighted, and the before/after numbers from the replay. That fit into one Slack thread and a short postmortem doc. No auditors are involved for a consumer feature like this.
9. Brazilian Portuguese users got English reply suggestions, which looks sloppy, and some of them probably turned the feature off. We don't know how many. The localization team spent a day triaging reports that turned out to be ours. No decisions or money were at stake. It was an embarrassment-level incident, not a harm-level one.
10. Honestly, the blind spot. The evals gave us false confidence because coverage for that locale and feature combination was thin. If a gap like that exists in a feature where the output matters more, like anything safety-adjacent, a day and a half of drift would be a much bigger deal.
11. The APM vendor's LLM observability for traces and token/latency metrics, the prompt and version registry, our offline eval harness, the canary dashboards, and a notebook for the replay. Logs were in our normal logging stack.
12. None of them told me the eval suite didn't cover that slice. That's the gap. The traces showed me exactly what happened once I looked. What I didn't have was anything saying "this change touches a code path your evals barely exercise." Coverage-aware gating is what I'd want, not better forensics.
13. We pay for the APM vendor's LLM product, we built the registry and the eval harness ourselves, and we've spent a lot of engineer time writing eval sets. None of it fully solves the problem because evals are only as good as the cases someone thought to write. That's a people and judgment problem more than a tooling problem.
14. My rough estimate: something user-visible gets past the gates maybe two or three times a year, and smaller things get caught in canary every few weeks. The last one took about 3 hours of active debugging and maybe 10 hours total including postmortem and the new eval slice. Most of the cost was the detection delay, not the debugging.
15. I'd talk to smaller teams that ship agents with lots of tool calls and don't have a mature eval setup. Also to people in regulated industries, like lending, insurance or healthcare, where someone outside engineering has to sign off on why a behaviour changed. Their pain probably looks very different from mine.
16. Straight answer: for this incident, not much. The tracing and the good-vs-bad diff are things my APM vendor and prompt registry already did well enough, and finding the divergent step took minutes once someone was looking. The controlled re-run piece is basically my notebook replay, so it's nice to have packaged, but I'd built it in an afternoon. The "verifiable evidence bundle" doesn't map to any need I have. Nobody asked me to prove anything cryptographically. Where I can see value is the confound question, ours versus the provider's model update. If you could separate that cleanly and automatically, that's interesting. But my main doubt is that you're solving the stage after detection, and detection was my problem.
17. Possibly, but with constraints. I can't share anything from our actual system, prompts or logs, and getting legal to approve even a sanitized version would take months. What I could realistically do on my own time is build a small synthetic pipeline: a public model, a toy retrieval step, a templated prompt, and a planted regression similar to the locale bug. I'd keep the cause to myself and see whether you find it. I'd want it understood that it reflects my experience, not my employer's system, and I'd only spend a few hours on it. Also, if your tool needs to be wired into the pipeline to record traces, that's fine for a toy, but it tells you nothing about whether we'd adopt it.

## S5 — Research engineer and OSS RAG-library maintainer

1. I co-maintain a mid-sized open-source Python library for building and evaluating retrieval-augmented generation pipelines. It covers chunking, retrieval and a set of eval metrics like context recall and answer faithfulness, and I also use it for my own research in a university NLP group.
2. The most recent one was a GitHub issue about two months ago titled roughly "faithfulness scores dropped after upgrading". The user said their average faithfulness went from about 0.78 to 0.66 after moving from our 0.8 to 0.9 release. They gave no config and no data, just a screenshot of two numbers. At almost the same time, one of our own students saw context recall drop a few points on a public QA benchmark, and I couldn't immediately tell whether that was the same problem or a different one.
3. We noticed because a user complained. Our CI only checks that the metrics compute and that a few tiny fixtures give stable values, so nothing failed. The release had been out for about nine days before the issue was filed. My guess is other people hit it earlier and never reported it, but I can't prove that.
4. First I opened our changelog and the diff between the two release tags, which was about 140 commits. Then I asked the user for a minimal reproduction, and it took them about four days to send a small notebook on a public dataset. I built two pinned environments, one for each version, and confirmed I could reproduce the drop. Then I git-bisected across our commits, rerunning a 200-question subset each time. The bisect pointed to a commit that changed our default sentence splitter to a newer version of a third-party segmentation dependency. That changed where chunks broke around abbreviations and lists. Even after reverting that, the user's numbers didn't fully recover. Eventually I found they had also upgraded their embedding library in the same environment, and the new version changed a normalization default.
5. Getting a usable repro took the longest in calendar time, and the bisect took the longest in my own time, because each step means a fresh environment and a few minutes of LLM-judge calls. The guesswork was separating the two causes. Once I knew there were two changes, I was eyeballing chunk boundaries in a notebook and arguing with myself about which one explained how much of the drop.
6. I swapped one factor at a time: our old splitter with their new embedding library, then the reverse, then both. That gave me a rough split, roughly two-thirds from our splitter and one-third from their embedding upgrade. I'd call that moderately sure, not very sure. The LLM judge has its own noise, and I only ran each configuration a couple of times, so I'd put the split at plus or minus a fair amount.
7. Partly. I released a patch that pinned the old segmentation behaviour as the default and made the new one opt-in. I also reran the user's notebook and our 200-question subset under both versions and got back to within about a point of the original numbers. The user confirmed on the issue that it looked fine. I never properly verified the embedding-library part on their side, I just documented it.
8. Mostly the other maintainer and the user. For the maintainer, a small table in the PR description was enough: four configurations and their scores, plus a link to the bisect commit. For the user, I posted the same table and a short explanation. Nobody like an auditor was involved. In academia, the nearest equivalent is a reviewer asking why your numbers don't match a previous paper.
9. Mostly people's trust in their own experiments. At least two users said they had been comparing retrieval setups across the upgrade boundary, so their comparisons were quietly confounded. In my group, the student's paper draft had a results table that mixed runs from both versions, and we had to rerun about a week of experiments. Nothing safety-critical, but it's the kind of thing that ends up in a published number.
10. Honestly, publishing wrong numbers, either in our own paper or because other people cite results computed with our library. A slow fix wasn't the risk. The risk was that nobody notices and a "method X beats method Y" claim is really just a chunking artifact. Reputationally, an eval library that silently shifts its metrics is in a bad spot.
11. Jupyter notebooks, pytest, pinned conda and requirements lockfiles, git bisect, and plain Python logging. Sometimes W&B for the research runs, and the student had used it, which helped a bit because we could see which runs had which package versions. No commercial tracing. The library itself only logs at debug level, and most users never turn that on.
12. Nothing told me where in the pipeline two runs started to differ. I had final scores and could dump chunks manually, but I had no per-step comparison along the lines of "same input, different chunks here, therefore different retrieved contexts, therefore a different judge verdict".
